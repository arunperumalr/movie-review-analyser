===REVIEW===

Sam J. Miller’s *Blackfish City* takes place in Qaanaaq, a floating Arctic city built after climate disasters reshaped
the world. The city’s separate sectors reflect sharp social divisions, with wealth and privilege concentrated among a
few while the rest struggle to survive under systems managed by algorithms and artificial intelligence. Into this uneasy
setting arrives a strange woman accompanied by an orca and a polar bear, a presence that immediately disrupts the
fragile balance of the city.The novel is packed with vivid imagery that feels both beautiful and unsettling. Miller
blends advanced technology with urban decay so naturally that Qaanaaq feels tangible — crowded food stalls under tarps,
grey skies hanging endlessly above the city, rusted machinery, and streets filled with desperation. The atmosphere
constantly shifts between awe and hopelessness, giving the world a distinct identity.The story moves at a measured pace,
unfolding through multiple viewpoints and scattered records that slowly reveal hidden tensions and forgotten histories.
These fragments add depth to the world while building toward larger confrontations beneath the surface. Beneath its
science fiction setting, the novel explores themes of inequality, marginalization, environmental collapse, and the
influence of wealth and power in a fractured society. Some sections drift into heavy exposition, but the world-building
remains consistently immersive. References to past revolutions, drowned nations, and political unrest make the setting
feel much larger than the immediate story. Like the best science fiction, *Blackfish City* leaves behind a
lingering sense of unease, presenting a future filled with technological brilliance, social decay, and people trying to
preserve hope in a world slowly falling apart.



===REVIEW===

What happens when a group of distinctive writer-directors known for unconventional cinema decide to create a film
together without holding back? The result feels very much like *Super Deluxe*. Directed by Thiagarajan Kumararaja, the
film constantly shifts between genres and tones while balancing multiple interconnected stories that somehow come
together with surprising confidence. The screenplay, written by several creative voices, packs the narrative with
eccentric characters, emotional breakdowns, dark humor, and moments of absurdity that remain strangely grounded. Each
storyline unfolds with its own rhythm, yet the film never feels lifeless or mechanical. Instead, it moves with chaotic
energy, jumping from uncomfortable comedy to emotional vulnerability in a matter of seconds. The performances elevate
the material tremendously. Vijay Sethupathi delivers one of the film’s most memorable performances as Shilpa, portraying
the character with restraint and humanity. Fahadh Faasil, Samantha Ruth Prabhu, Mysskin, and Ramya Krishnan each bring
intensity and personality to their roles, making even the film’s strangest moments believable. Visually, the film stands
out with striking cinematography, stylized production design, and vibrant color palettes that create an almost
dreamlike atmosphere. Yuvan Shankar Raja keeps the background score subtle for most parts, allowing silence and
awkwardness to carry many scenes before suddenly introducing nostalgic musical touches. The dialogue is filled with
clever observations and darkly comic exchanges, many of which linger long after the scene ends. At nearly three hours,
the film occasionally slows down under the weight of its ambition, but its emotional and thematic payoffs justify the
journey. *Super Deluxe* moves through themes of identity, morality, faith, relationships, desire, social judgment, and
existential confusion with fearless unpredictability. It is messy, hilarious, unsettling, emotional, and completely
committed to its own madness — a film that overwhelms you in the best possible way.

“I” might still work for you if you enjoy watching committed actors physically transform themselves for demanding roles,
admire visually extravagant filmmaking and lavish production values, appreciate large-scale cinematic spectacle driven
by relentless promotion, are willing to ignore clunky dialogue and shallow emotional writing, accept outdated
stereotypes played for easy laughs, find repetitive humiliation humor entertaining, enjoy predictable revenge arcs and
conveniently hidden villains, and continue to believe that technical polish alone is enough to define “world-class”
cinema despite weak storytelling beneath the surface.
